02 / Core notes

Think in dependencies and service outcomes.

Job definitions

A job definition describes executable work and its runtime settings. Use reusable standards for ownership, naming, alerts, and recovery.

Conditions and dependencies

Conditions express predecessor and successor relationships. Design the flow so downstream jobs wait for the correct upstream state.

Calendars and time

Business calendars, scheduling rules, and time zones determine when work is eligible. Check daylight-saving and holiday behavior where relevant.

Operations

Monitor order status, alerts, SLA risk, and failure history. Define when to rerun, hold, or escalate rather than blindly restarting a chain.

03 / Interview practice

Questions and answer frameworks.

How do you model dependencies in a workflow?

Represent each job with explicit predecessor and successor conditions, then check that every path has a valid completion or failure route. Avoid hidden timing assumptions where a dependency is clearer.

How should a failed job be rerun?

Determine the failure cause and whether the job is safe to repeat. Confirm upstream inputs, downstream completion state, and partial writes; rerun from a documented recovery point and reconcile outputs.

What would you inspect when a workflow misses its SLA?

Review job durations, queue or resource waits, late predecessors, calendar rules, alerts, and recent changes. Separate a slow job from a dependency delay, then address the measured bottleneck.

Why are calendars and time zones important?

They determine eligible run times across business days, holidays, daylight-saving changes, and distributed teams. Validate schedules against the business time zone and downstream deadlines.
